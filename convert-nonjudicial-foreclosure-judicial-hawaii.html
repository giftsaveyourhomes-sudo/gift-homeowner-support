Can You Force Your Hawaiʻi Foreclosure Into Court?

Most foreclosures in Hawaiʻi happen non-judicially — meaning entirely outside the court system, through a "power of sale" process spelled out in the mortgage itself. That surprises a lot of homeowners, who assume a judge is automatically involved. If you're an owner-occupant, Hawaiʻi law may give you a path to change that.

Why it matters whether a judge is involved

In a non-judicial foreclosure, the process moves forward through notices and a scheduled sale, without a judge reviewing the case unless you personally file something in court to challenge it. In a judicial foreclosure, the lender has to sue you, a judge oversees the case, and you generally have a formal opportunity to respond and raise any defenses before a sale can happen. That's a meaningfully different experience — more structure, more time built into the process, and a neutral party reviewing what's happening.

The conversion option for owner-occupants

Hawaiʻi law generally provides a mechanism — sometimes referred to as "conversion to judicial foreclosure" — that allows an owner-occupant facing a non-judicial foreclosure to petition to have the case moved into the court system instead. The idea is to give homeowners who live in the property being foreclosed on a meaningful opportunity for judicial review, even when the lender chose the faster non-judicial route.

This generally isn't automatic, and it isn't something that happens by simply asking informally — it involves specific procedures, deadlines, and requirements tied to your status as an owner-occupant. The exact window to act and the paperwork involved are the kind of details that matter enormously and that I'm not going to guess at here.

The catch: how this interacts with mediation programs

Hawaiʻi has also offered foreclosure dispute-resolution and mediation programs over the years. How electing into one of those programs interacts with your conversion rights is exactly the kind of nuance that can trip people up — in some circumstances, choosing one path may affect what's still available to you on another. This is not a decision to make based on a general article; it's one to make after someone who knows the current rules has looked at your specific notice and timeline.

What to do if this applies to you

  • Check whether you're facing a non-judicial foreclosure — your notice documents or your servicer can confirm which type of process you're in.
  • Confirm you qualify as an owner-occupant — this generally matters for which protections and options apply to you.
  • Don't wait to ask about this. Anything involving a petition to a court is time-sensitive, and missing a filing window can close the door entirely.
  • Get a professional to walk through your actual notice with you before deciding whether to pursue conversion, mediation, or another path — the right move depends on details specific to your situation.
