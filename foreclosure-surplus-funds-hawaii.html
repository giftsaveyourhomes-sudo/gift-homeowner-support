Did Your Hawaiʻi Home Sell for More Than You Owed? You May Be Owed the Difference

If your home already went through a foreclosure sale, it's natural to assume that chapter is simply closed. But if your home sold at auction for more than what was owed on your mortgage and other liens, that difference — called surplus or excess proceeds — doesn't automatically belong to the lender. In many cases, it belongs to you, and it's worth finding out whether anyone ever claimed it.

Where the surplus actually goes

When a foreclosed home sells at auction for more than what's owed, that money gets distributed in order of priority: the foreclosing lender gets paid what it's owed first, then any junior lienholders — a second mortgage, an HOA lien, a judgment lien — get paid in order, if they exist. Whatever is left after everyone with a valid claim ahead of you has been paid is generally the former homeowner's to claim.

Who actually holds the money

For a non-judicial foreclosure sale (the more common type in Hawaiʻi), the trustee who conducted the sale typically holds any surplus initially. If there are competing claims to the money, or if it goes unclaimed, it may later be deposited with the circuit court. For a judicial foreclosure, the court itself is generally involved in overseeing distribution of any surplus from the start.

There is a deadline — don't assume you have unlimited time

Surplus funds aren't held forever. Depending on the circumstances, unclaimed funds can eventually be turned over to the state's unclaimed property program, or become harder to claim the longer they sit. Exactly how long you have and what the formal process looks like can depend on how your specific sale was handled — this is genuinely one of those areas where the details matter, and you should confirm the current, exact timeline for your situation directly with the trustee or circuit court clerk rather than assuming based on anything general you read, including this article.

How to find out if there's money waiting for you

  • Contact the trustee who conducted your sale directly and ask whether any surplus exists and whether it's been claimed.
  • Pull your trustee's deed or sale documents from the Bureau of Conveyances to see the final sale price compared to what was owed.
  • If your case went through a judicial foreclosure, check with the circuit court clerk for the case.
  • Hawaiʻi's unclaimed property program is worth checking as well, in case funds were already turned over there.

Watch out for surplus-recovery companies

Because foreclosure sale records are public, some companies specifically search for homeowners who may be owed surplus funds and offer to "recover" the money — for a large percentage fee, sometimes 20% or more. In many cases, this is information and a process you can pursue yourself at little or no cost by contacting the trustee or court clerk directly. Before agreeing to give up a significant cut of money that may already legally be yours, it's worth spending a little time finding out what it actually takes to claim it directly.

What you'll generally need

  • Proof you owned the property at the time of the sale (your deed or title documents)
  • Government-issued identification
  • If the original owner has passed away, probate or estate documentation showing your right to claim on their behalf

Getting the right help

Whether surplus exists in your specific case, exactly how to claim it, and what the current deadline is are all things that depend on the details of your sale. I can help you understand the bigger picture and point you toward the trustee, court clerk, or attorney who can confirm your specific situation — before a surplus-recovery company offers to take a large cut of money you may be able to claim yourself.
