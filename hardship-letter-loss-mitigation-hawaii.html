Your Loss Mitigation Application: What Actually Belongs in a Hardship Letter

A lot of homeowners applying for a loan modification or forbearance treat it like a phone call — explain what happened, ask for help, wait to hear back. In reality, it's a documented application, and the hardship letter inside it is often the weakest part. A vague or incomplete one is one of the most common, fixable reasons an application stalls or gets denied.

What a loss mitigation application package actually includes

Beyond the hardship letter itself, servicers generally want to see proof of income (pay stubs, benefit statements, or profit-and-loss if self-employed), recent bank statements, and often a basic household budget or financial worksheet. Missing even one piece is enough for a servicer to mark the application incomplete, which restarts the clock.

What the hardship letter actually needs to do

A hardship letter isn't a place to express frustration or plead generally — it's meant to clearly answer a few specific questions for the person reviewing your file:

  • What happened? Name the specific event — job loss, reduced hours, a medical emergency, divorce, death of a household earner, a significant unexpected expense. Vague language like "things have been hard" doesn't give a reviewer anything to work with.
  • When did it happen? A specific date or timeframe helps connect the hardship to when payments actually became difficult.
  • Is it resolved or ongoing? This matters because it affects which options actually make sense — a temporary hardship points toward forbearance, while a permanent change in income points toward a modification.
  • What are you asking for? State it directly — a modification, a repayment plan, forbearance. Don't make the reviewer guess.

Common mistakes that stall or sink an application

  • Being vague instead of specific. "We've had financial difficulties" tells a reviewer nothing actionable.
  • Leaving it unsigned or undated. Small, easily missed details that are enough to trigger an incomplete-application notice.
  • Submitting incomplete financial documentation. This is the single most common reason applications sit in limbo.
  • Not confirming it was received. Mail and fax submissions can get lost — always confirm receipt and keep your own copy of everything.
  • Going silent after submitting. If you're asked for additional documents, respond quickly — delays on your end can restart timelines.

A structure that works

A clear hardship letter generally covers, in order: what happened and when, how it affected your ability to make payments, whether the situation is temporary or ongoing, and specifically what you're requesting. Keep it factual and concise — a page is usually plenty.

What happens after you submit, and the timelines that apply

Under federal mortgage servicing rules, once a servicer receives your application, they're generally required to acknowledge it within a matter of days and tell you specifically whether it's considered complete — and if it isn't, what's still missing and by when you need to provide it. Once your application is actually complete, servicers are generally required to evaluate you for all available options and send a written decision within about 30 days, provided it was submitted with enough time before any scheduled foreclosure sale. Exact timing requirements and deadlines can vary by circumstance — if a deadline seems to have passed without a response, that's worth raising directly with your servicer or a HUD-approved housing counselor right away.
