Co-Signed a Mortgage for a Family Member? Here's What Happens If They Fall Behind

With home prices what they are on Oʻahu, it's common for a parent to co-sign so an adult child can qualify for a mortgage, or for one family member to help another get into a home. What's less understood is what co-signing actually obligates you to — and what it means for you specifically if the primary borrower later falls behind.

What co-signing actually means

When you co-sign a mortgage, you're not just vouching for someone — you're taking on equal legal responsibility for the debt, whether or not you live in the home or hold title to it. If the primary borrower stops paying, the lender can pursue you for the full remaining balance, not just a share of it. This often surprises co-signers who assumed their role was more of a formality.

How it affects your own credit

The loan shows up on your credit report too, not just the primary borrower's. Late or missed payments can lower your credit score just as much as theirs — even if you had no idea a payment was missed. It also counts against your own debt-to-income ratio, which can make it harder for you to qualify for your own mortgage or other credit later, regardless of your actual payment history on anything else.

What happens if the primary borrower falls behind

  • The lender can pursue either or both of you for the payment — being a co-signer doesn't put you second in line.
  • You may not be automatically notified when payments are missed, depending on the servicer's practices — it's worth proactively asking to be kept informed if you're a co-signer on someone else's loan.
  • If it progresses to foreclosure, that affects your credit report as well as the primary borrower's, even if your name was never on title.

What a co-signer can actually do

  • Talk to the primary borrower early and directly if you suspect or learn they're struggling — the earlier a problem is addressed, the more options generally exist.
  • Ask the servicer about being included in communications — as a co-obligor on the loan, you generally have a right to information about its status.
  • Understand that you generally can't simply remove yourself from the loan. Getting off a mortgage you co-signed usually requires the primary borrower to refinance into their name alone — something that isn't always possible, especially if their financial situation is what caused the problem in the first place.
  • Weigh your own position carefully if payments are missed — protecting your own credit and the family relationship can pull in different directions, and there's no single right answer.

Before you co-sign for someone else

If you're considering co-signing for a family member, it's worth having a direct conversation beforehand about what happens if their income or situation changes — and going in with clear eyes that you're taking on real financial risk, not just paperwork. Knowing upfront that you generally can't easily undo a co-signature later makes it a decision worth thinking through carefully.
