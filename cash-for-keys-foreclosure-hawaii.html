Cash for Keys After Foreclosure: What Hawaiʻi Homeowners Should Know

If your home has already sold at a foreclosure auction, or you're close to it, you may get a call offering you money to move out on your own timeline instead of going through a formal eviction. This is commonly called "cash for keys," and understanding it can save you both money and stress.

What cash for keys actually is

Cash for keys is a voluntary agreement where the new owner (often the bank or an investor who bought the property at auction) pays the former homeowner to move out by an agreed date, leave the property in reasonably good condition, and turn over the keys — instead of the new owner having to go through a formal eviction process. It can also come up earlier, as part of a deed-in-lieu-of-foreclosure agreement, as relocation assistance for leaving voluntarily.

Why it can actually work in your favor

A formal eviction takes time, costs the new owner money in legal fees, and creates uncertainty for both sides. Offering a former homeowner cash to leave on a set date is often cheaper and faster for them — which means you may have real negotiating room. This isn't charity; it's a transaction where your cooperation and timeline have value.

What it's generally worth

Amounts vary widely based on the property, the new owner, and local market conditions — there's no fixed number I can give you that applies everywhere. What matters more than any specific figure is treating the first offer as a starting point, not a final one, and understanding what you're agreeing to before you say yes.

What to watch for before you sign anything

  • Get it in writing. A verbal promise of payment isn't enforceable — the move-out date, the payment amount, and when payment happens should all be spelled out in a signed agreement.
  • Don't move out before you're paid (or before payment terms you're comfortable with are met) — once you've vacated, your leverage is gone.
  • Understand the condition requirements. Agreements often require leaving the property "broom clean" or in similar condition — know what's expected so payment isn't disputed later.
  • Confirm who you're actually dealing with. Verify the person offering you this is actually authorized to represent the new owner, especially if contacted out of the blue.
  • Know your timeline either way. Understanding your actual legal deadline to vacate (with or without an agreement) helps you negotiate from an informed position instead of out of fear.

Getting the right help
