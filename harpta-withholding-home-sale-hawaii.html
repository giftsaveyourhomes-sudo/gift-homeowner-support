HARPTA: The Withholding Surprise When Selling a Hawaiʻi Home

If you're selling a home or inherited property in Hawaiʻi and you're not a Hawaiʻi resident, there's a state withholding requirement that can take a real bite out of your proceeds at closing — before you ever see a dime. It's called HARPTA, and it surprises a lot of sellers who've never heard of it until they're sitting at the closing table.

What HARPTA actually is

HARPTA (the Hawaiʻi Real Property Tax Act) requires a withholding of generally 7.25% of the gross sale price — not your profit, not your net proceeds, the full sale price — when a non-resident seller sells Hawaiʻi real property. It isn't an extra tax on top of what you owe; it's an advance payment toward whatever Hawaiʻi capital gains tax you may actually owe, similar in concept to how a paycheck withholds income tax in advance.

Why it catches people off guard

Because it's calculated on the gross sale price rather than your actual gain, the amount withheld at closing can be far more than what you'll ultimately owe in tax — sometimes dramatically more, especially if you have little or no actual profit on the sale. That money isn't gone forever; you can generally file to get back whatever you overpaid. But that means waiting on a refund instead of having full access to your sale proceeds right when you need them.

Who it applies to

  • Non-resident sellers. If you can establish Hawaiʻi residency, you're generally exempt from the withholding requirement.
  • Heirs who inherited a Hawaiʻi property but live elsewhere. This comes up often — someone inherits a parent's Oʻahu home, lives on the mainland, and is surprised by HARPTA withholding when they sell it.
  • Sellers who recently moved away (or recently moved here). Residency for this purpose isn't just about where you currently live — it can depend on specific timing tests, so don't assume either way without confirming.

If you're also not a U.S. resident

Foreign sellers may also face a separate federal withholding (FIRPTA) on top of HARPTA — meaning two different withholdings stacked on the same sale. If this applies to you, getting professional tax guidance before closing is especially important.

What you can do about it

  • Don't assume your full sale price is coming to you at closing if you're a non-resident seller — plan your finances around the withheld amount being temporarily unavailable.
  • Ask about exemption or reduced-withholding paperwork. There are state forms for claiming exemptions or requesting a reduced withholding amount in certain situations — whether you qualify depends on your specific facts.
  • Talk to a CPA or tax professional before you're at the closing table, not after — there may be steps available to reduce what's withheld or speed up getting an overpayment back.
  • Factor this into your decision if you're weighing whether to sell a distressed or inherited Hawaiʻi property — the cash you expect at closing may be meaningfully less than the sale price if HARPTA applies to you.
