Zombie Second Mortgages in Hawaiʻi: When an Old, Forgotten Loan Resurfaces

Years ago, a lot of homeowners took out a second mortgage or home equity line of credit, fell behind during hard times, and simply never heard from that lender again. Many assumed it was written off, forgiven, or forgotten. Then, years later, a letter arrives — sometimes from a completely different company — saying the debt is being collected, and a foreclosure could follow. This is what's known as a "zombie" second mortgage, and it's a real and growing problem.

How a debt goes "zombie" in the first place

During and after the 2008 housing crisis, many second mortgages and home equity lines of credit went unpaid alongside primary mortgages. In a lot of cases, the primary mortgage was addressed — through modification, foreclosure, or otherwise — but the second lien technically remained. Some of these debts were written off the original lender's books for accounting purposes and sold to debt buyers for pennies on the dollar, sometimes multiple times over the years, while the homeowner heard nothing and reasonably assumed it was resolved.

Why they resurface now

  • Home values recovered. A debt buyer that paid very little for an old, seemingly worthless lien has a much stronger incentive to collect once the underlying home has real equity again.
  • The lien never actually went away. Unless it was formally released, satisfied, or discharged, the lien can still legally attach to the property even after years of silence.
  • Debt buyers specialize in this. Some companies specifically buy portfolios of old, dormant second liens because of exactly this pattern — quiet acquisition, then a wave of collection or foreclosure activity once conditions favor it.

Why this feels — and can be — unfair

Many homeowners genuinely believed these old debts were gone, made no payments for years without any contact from a lender, and made major financial decisions assuming the debt no longer existed. There are real legal defenses that sometimes apply in these situations, including statute of limitations arguments (a legal time limit on how long a debt can be collected or a lien enforced) — but whether one applies depends heavily on the specific facts, timeline, and how Hawaiʻi law treats your particular situation.

What to do if this happens to you

  • Don't ignore it, and don't panic. Ignoring a foreclosure notice doesn't make it go away, but neither does immediate payment without understanding your rights.
  • Request full documentation. You're entitled to ask the collector to verify the debt, show chain of ownership, and prove they have the legal right to collect and foreclose.
  • Don't acknowledge or restart the debt casually. In some cases, even a partial payment or written acknowledgment can restart the clock on an otherwise time-barred debt — talk to an attorney before responding directly.
  • Get a real estate or consumer protection attorney involved early. This is a legal fight with real defenses, not something to negotiate alone.
