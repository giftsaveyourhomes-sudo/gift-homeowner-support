Your Right to a Single Point of Contact at Your Mortgage Servicer

If you've ever called your mortgage servicer about a hardship and had to explain your whole situation to a different person every single time, you've felt exactly what federal rules were written to prevent. If you're behind on your mortgage, you generally have a right to a dedicated point of contact — and knowing that can save you a lot of frustration.

What this right actually covers

Federal mortgage servicing rules generally require servicers to assign a specific person or team — not a random rep from a call queue — to delinquent borrowers who are working through their options. That contact is supposed to be able to access your file, answer your questions, and help you submit paperwork, instead of you starting from zero on every call.

Why this matters so much

Loss mitigation (loan modifications, repayment plans, forbearance) runs on paperwork and deadlines. When you get bounced between different reps who don't know your file, documents get lost, instructions contradict each other, and deadlines slip through the cracks — not because you did anything wrong, but because nobody had the full picture. A real single point of contact is supposed to fix exactly that problem.

"Dual tracking" — another protection worth knowing

Federal rules also generally restrict something called "dual tracking" — where a servicer moves forward with foreclosure at the same time it's supposed to be reviewing your complete application for help. If you've submitted a complete loss mitigation application, there are generally protections meant to prevent the foreclosure process from quietly advancing in the background while your request sits unreviewed, as long as you've applied with enough time before a scheduled sale.

What to do with this information

  • Ask directly: "Who is my single point of contact?" — get a name and direct contact information, not just a general phone line.
  • Keep a record of who you spoke with and when, especially if you're not getting consistent answers — this matters if you need to escalate later.
  • If you submit a complete application, get confirmation it's complete — an incomplete application generally doesn't carry the same protections.
  • If you suspect a scheduled sale is moving forward despite a pending complete application, that's a serious red flag worth raising with a HUD-approved housing counselor or attorney right away.

Getting the right help

I'm not an attorney, and exactly how these federal protections apply — including specific timing windows and what counts as a "complete" application — depends on details I can't evaluate for you here.
