Your Home Was Damaged by a Disaster — Here's What Happens to Your Mortgage

Hurricanes, flash floods, and wildfire risk are all real possibilities for a home in Hawaiʻi. If a disaster damages your property, dealing with the aftermath is overwhelming enough without also worrying about your mortgage. The good news most people don't realize: real, structured relief exists specifically for homeowners in this situation — you just have to know it's there and ask for it.

It starts with a disaster declaration

Most of the mortgage protections below only kick in once your area has an official federal disaster declaration — typically issued after a hurricane, major flood, wildfire, or similar event. This declaration is what activates the specific relief programs tied to FHA, VA, and conventional (Fannie Mae/Freddie Mac) loans.

The foreclosure pause you may not know about

Once your area is under a federal disaster declaration, FHA, VA, Fannie Mae, and Freddie Mac loans generally authorize servicers to suspend foreclosure proceedings for around 90 days. If you're already behind on your mortgage and a disaster hits, this pause buys real time — but it isn't always automatic. Contact your servicer directly, explain that your property is in the declared disaster area, and ask specifically about the foreclosure moratorium.

Disaster-specific forbearance

Beyond the foreclosure pause, disaster-related forbearance is often easier to get than standard hardship forbearance. For example, some investors allow servicers to grant an initial forbearance period of a few months based simply on the disaster itself, without requiring the full hardship documentation process upfront. This means you may be able to pause or reduce payments quickly while you deal with more urgent things like housing and repairs.

How your insurance claim interacts with your mortgage

This catches a lot of homeowners off guard: because your lender has a financial interest in your home, they're typically listed on your homeowners insurance policy as a "mortgagee" or loss payee. For a significant claim, the insurance check may be made out to both you and your lender jointly, and larger payouts are often disbursed in stages tied to completed repair work rather than as a single lump sum. This isn't your lender trying to control your money out of distrust — it's standard practice to make sure insurance proceeds actually go toward repairing the home that secures the loan.

What to do right after a disaster damages your home

  • Document everything immediately — photos, videos, and a written account of the damage before any cleanup begins.
  • File your insurance claim promptly and ask specifically how the payout process works if your lender is a joint payee.
  • Contact your mortgage servicer directly — don't wait for them to reach out. Tell them your property is in the disaster area and ask about both the foreclosure pause and forbearance options.
  • Keep records of every call and agreement, including names, dates, and what was promised.
  • Look into FEMA individual assistance if your area qualifies — it's a separate program from your mortgage relief but can help with immediate needs.

Getting the right help

The specific relief available to you depends on your loan type, your servicer's policies, and whether your area has an active disaster declaration.
