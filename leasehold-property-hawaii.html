Leasehold vs. Fee Simple in Hawaiʻi: What Every Leasehold Owner Should Know

Hawaiʻi has a real estate arrangement that catches a lot of people off guard, including people who've owned their home for years without fully understanding it: leasehold property. If you own a leasehold home, you own the structure — but not the land underneath it. That distinction creates a risk most mainland homeowners never have to think about, and it's separate from anything your mortgage lender controls.

Fee simple vs. leasehold, in plain terms

  • Fee simple means you own the land and the structure on it outright. This is what most people picture when they think of owning a home.
  • Leasehold means you own the home itself, but the land it sits on is owned by someone else — a private landowner, a trust, or an estate — and you pay ground lease rent for the right to use that land, typically under a long-term lease.

Leasehold arrangements are more common in Hawaiʻi than almost anywhere else in the country, particularly in certain older condo developments and some single-family home communities.

Why this matters beyond your mortgage

Your mortgage and your ground lease are two completely separate obligations. You can be perfectly current on your mortgage payment and still be at risk if you fall behind on lease rent — because the lease is a separate contract with the landowner, not your mortgage lender. Falling behind on ground rent can eventually put your right to the property at risk, independent of anything happening with your loan.

Things every leasehold owner should know

  • Lease rent can renegotiate. Many ground leases include periodic renegotiation of the rent amount, sometimes resulting in significant increases that catch owners off guard if they weren't tracking the lease terms.
  • The lease has an end date. Leasehold agreements run for a set term. What happens at the end — renewal, conversion to fee simple, or the land reverting to the landowner — depends entirely on your specific lease and any conversion laws that may apply.
  • Financing can be different. Some lenders treat leasehold properties differently than fee simple ones, and remaining lease term can affect your ability to refinance or a future buyer's ability to get a mortgage at all.
  • Fee simple conversion has happened before. Hawaiʻi has, at points, had laws allowing leasehold owners to purchase the fee simple interest in certain residential leasehold properties — whether that applies to your situation is worth checking with an attorney familiar with your specific lease and property type.

If you're behind on lease rent

Treat it with the same urgency as a mortgage default — it's a separate but equally real risk to your homeownership. Contact the landowner or lease administrator directly, understand what remedies exist under your specific lease, and don't assume being current on your mortgage protects you here. A real estate attorney familiar with Hawaiʻi leasehold law can tell you exactly what your lease allows and what your options are.

Getting the right help
