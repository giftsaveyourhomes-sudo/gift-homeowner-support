Your Adjustable-Rate Mortgage Is About to Reset — What Happens to Your Payment?

If you have an adjustable-rate mortgage (ARM), the low fixed rate you started with doesn't last the life of the loan. At some point it "resets" to a variable rate — and on Oʻahu, where loan balances tend to run high, even a modest rate increase can mean a real jump in your monthly payment.

How an ARM reset actually works

Most ARMs start with a fixed-rate period — often 3, 5, 7, or 10 years — after which the rate adjusts based on a market index plus a margin set by your lender. From that point on, your rate (and payment) can move up or down at set intervals, generally within limits called caps: an initial cap on the first adjustment, a periodic cap on later adjustments, and a lifetime cap on how high the rate can ever go. The specific index, margin, and caps that apply to you are spelled out in your original loan documents — they vary loan to loan, so there's no single number that applies to everyone.

Why this can hit harder here

A rate increase of even a point or two sounds small, but on a loan balance that's common on Oʻahu, that can translate into a payment increase of several hundred dollars a month. Because the dollar impact scales with the loan amount, the same rate movement that might be a minor adjustment elsewhere can meaningfully strain a monthly budget here.

What to do before your reset date

  • Find your reset date and the terms that apply to it. Your original loan documents (or your servicer, if you can't locate them) will show the index, margin, and caps for your specific loan.
  • Ask your servicer for a written payment estimate based on current index values, so you're not caught off guard by the actual number.
  • Look into refinancing into a fixed rate before the reset, if you qualify — this is generally easier to arrange while you're current on payments and have lead time, rather than after the new payment has already strained your budget.
  • Start adjusting your budget now for the higher possible payment, even if you're also exploring refinancing — plans fall through, and it's better to be prepared either way.

If the new payment is already unaffordable

If you've run the numbers and the reset payment genuinely doesn't work for your budget, you still generally have options — loan modification, a different refinance structure, or a repayment plan if you've already fallen behind. The key is not waiting until after you've missed payments to start that conversation; loss mitigation options are usually easier to access while you're still current.
